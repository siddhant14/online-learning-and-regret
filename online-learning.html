Part 1: Online learning and regret

Suppose several predictors give advice before each outcome is known. We would like to combine their advice without having to identify the best predictor in advance. Multiplicative weights does this by repeatedly changing a probability distribution over the predictors. Its regret guarantee will later become a tool for proving a theorem about randomized algorithms.

The prediction game

An expert is simply a prediction rule. It could be a model, a person, or a rule such as “predict the same outcome as last time.” The mathematical framework does not assume that any expert is especially knowledgeable, or even that one predicts well.

For a binary prediction problem, the outcome and each expert’s prediction belong to {0,1}\{0,1\}. On each round:

  1. The learner receives the experts’ predictions.
  2. The learner chooses its prediction before seeing the outcome.
  3. The outcome is revealed, so the learner can determine which experts were wrong.
  4. The learner updates the influence given to each expert.

This is a full-information problem: after the outcome, every expert’s loss is available. We do not only observe the loss of the expert we followed.

It is useful to state the analysis in a slightly more general form. There are N≥2N\ge2 experts and T≥1T\ge1 rounds. Expert ii receives a loss ℓi,t∈[0,1]\ell_{i,t}\in[0,1] on round tt. In the binary example, this loss is 1 for a mistake and 0 for a correct prediction. Allowing fractional losses will let us use the same theorem for a matrix game in Part 2.

What regret measures

Without assumptions on the outcomes, expecting few mistakes in absolute terms is too much. Instead, we compare our performance with the available experts. If they all perform poorly, the learner is allowed to perform poorly too.

Cumulative loss and regret

The cumulative loss of expert ii is

Li,T=∑t=1Tℓi,t. L_{i,T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{i,t}.

If the learner uses a probability vector pt=(p1,t,…,pN,t)p_t=(p_{1,t},\ldots,p_{N,t}), define its mixture loss by

ℓ^t=∑i=1Npi,tℓi,t,L^T=∑t=1Tℓ^t. \widehat\ell_t=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i,t}\ell_{i,t}, \qquad \widehat L_T=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat\ell_t.

Its regret against the best fixed expert is

RT=L^T−min⁡1≤i≤NLi,T. R_T=\widehat L_T-\min_{1\le i\le N}L_{i,T}.

“Fixed” matters. We choose one expert in hindsight and count that expert’s loss over all TT rounds. We do not get to choose a different expert after each outcome. For example, if the expert totals are 10, 14, and 9, then the benchmark is 9. A learner total of 11 gives regret 2.

For a general prediction rule, regret can be negative: a learner may outperform every fixed expert. Whether this can happen depends on the rule. The fixed-rate exponential-weights algorithm analyzed below, with equal initial weights, has nonnegative cumulative mixture regret.

A small regret also does not imply a small loss. A learner with loss 102 against a best expert with loss 100 has regret 2, even though it still made many mistakes.

To implement the mixture as a randomized prediction, sample expert ii with probability pi,tp_{i,t} and follow its prediction. Then ℓ^t\widehat\ell_t is its conditional expected loss. This interpretation assumes that the losses are chosen without seeing the current sampled expert. An opponent may know the probability vector, but cannot wait to see the random choice before choosing the outcome.

The weight update

Start each expert with weight 1. Before a round, normalize the weights into probabilities. After the losses are revealed, multiply an expert’s weight by a factor that decreases with its loss:

Exponential weights

Choose a fixed learning rate η>0\eta>0. Initialize wi,1=1w_{i,1}=1. On each round tt, use

Wt=∑j=1Nwj,t,pi,t=wi,tWt. W_t=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j,t}, \qquad p_{i,t}=\frac{w_{i,t}}{W_t}.

After observing all losses, update

wi,t+1=wi,te−ηℓi,t. w_{i,t+1}=w_{i,t}e^{-\eta\ell_{i,t}}.

In the binary case, correct experts keep their weights and mistaken experts receive the penalty factor β=e−η\beta=e^{-\eta}. A larger learning rate makes a mistake more expensive. A smaller rate changes the distribution more gradually.

Repeated multiplication gives a useful alternative expression:

wi,t=e−ηLi,t−1. w_{i,t}=e^{-\eta L_{i,t-1}}.

The weight before round tt depends only on earlier losses. It does not use the outcome that is about to arrive.

How Weighted Majority fits

Deterministic Weighted Majority uses a weighted vote: it predicts the label receiving at least half the total weight, with a specified rule for ties. Randomized weighted prediction instead follows a sampled expert. They share the multiplicative update, but their predictions and guarantees differ.

The distinction is essential against adversarial outcomes. If an opponent always chooses the opposite of a deterministic binary prediction, that learner makes a mistake on every round. Of the always-0 and always-1 experts, at least one makes at most half as many mistakes. Randomization changes which guarantees are possible when the opponent cannot see the current random choice.

Our main analysis is for exponential weights and its mixture loss. The simulation includes deterministic voting for comparison; its displayed mistake bound is a separate guarantee.

The regret bound

Exponential-weights regret guarantee

For every sequence of full-information loss vectors in [0,1]N[0,1]^N, exponential weights with equal initial weights and fixed η>0\eta>0 satisfies

RT≤ln⁡Nη+ηT8. \boxed{R_T\le \frac{\ln N}{\eta}+\frac{\eta T}{8}.}

For a known horizon TT, choosing η=8ln⁡N/T\eta=\sqrt{8\ln N/T} gives

RT≤Tln⁡N2. R_T\le\sqrt{\frac{T\ln N}{2}}.

The two terms explain the learning-rate tradeoff. A small η\eta makes ln⁡N/η\ln N/\eta large; a large η\eta makes ηT/8\eta T/8 large. The stated choice balances them. For fixed NN, divide by TT:

RTT≤ln⁡N2T⟶0. \frac{R_T}{T}\le\sqrt{\frac{\ln N}{2T}}\longrightarrow0.

This is the meaning of sublinear regret: the average extra loss compared with the best fixed expert vanishes. This conclusion uses a rate tuned to the horizon, or another suitable learning-rate schedule. Keeping one positive constant rate forever does not make the displayed bound on average regret tend to zero.

The bound is the exponential-weights guarantee in Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, Theorem 2.2, applied to mixture loss. The binary randomized-prediction interpretation uses the protocol above.

Proof using total weight

Tracking each probability separately would make the analysis cumbersome. Instead, we track the total weight WtW_t. It connects the best expert with the learner: the best expert supplies a lower bound on total weight, while the learner’s losses control how quickly that total decreases.

A supporting exponential-moment lemma

Hoeffding’s lemma

If XX takes values in [0,1][0,1], then for every real ss,

ln⁡E[esX]≤s E[X]+s28. \ln\mathbb E[e^{sX}] \le s\,\mathbb E[X]+\frac{s^2}{8}.

This is an exponential-moment statement. The tail bound used for error reduction in the class notes is a related statement, not the same formula.

Proof of the lemma for a finite distribution

Write g(s)=ln⁡∑ipiesxig(s)=\ln\sum_i p_i e^{s x_i}, where 0≤xi≤10\le x_i\le1. Then g(0)=0g(0)=0 and g′(0)=∑ipixi=E[X]g'(0)=\sum_i p_i x_i=\mathbb E[X]. Differentiating twice shows that g′′(s)g''(s) is the variance of XX under the tilted probabilities

pi(s)=piesxi∑jpjesxj. p_i(s)=\frac{p_i e^{s x_i}}{\sum_jp_j e^{s x_j}}.

Every distribution on [0,1][0,1] has variance at most 1/41/4, since

Var⁡(X)≤E[(X−12)2]≤14. \operatorname{Var}(X) \le\mathbb E[(X-\tfrac12)^2]\le\tfrac14.

Taylor’s theorem, with g′′≤1/4g''\le1/4, gives g(s)≤g(0)+sg′(0)+s2/8g(s)\le g(0)+s g'(0)+s^2/8, proving the claim. This short supporting proof uses elementary differentiation; the rest of the regret proof is algebra.

Lower bound: keep the best expert’s contribution

After TT rounds, expert ii has weight e−ηLi,Te^{-\eta L_{i,T}}. Total weight includes this term for every expert, so

WT+1≥e−ηmin⁡iLi,T. W_{T+1}\ge e^{-\eta\min_i L_{i,T}}.

Since W1=NW_1=N, taking logarithms gives

ln⁡WT+1W1≥−ηmin⁡iLi,T−ln⁡N. \ln\frac{W_{T+1}}{W_1} \ge-\eta\min_i L_{i,T}-\ln N.

This step prevents total weight from shrinking faster than the best expert’s remaining weight allows.

Upper bound: measure the decrease each round

The update and normalization give

Wt+1Wt=∑ipi,te−ηℓi,t. \frac{W_{t+1}}{W_t} =\sum_i p_{i,t}e^{-\eta\ell_{i,t}}.

Apply Hoeffding’s lemma to the loss of an expert sampled from ptp_t, with s=−ηs=-\eta:

ln⁡Wt+1Wt≤−ηℓ^t+η28. \ln\frac{W_{t+1}}{W_t} \le-\eta\widehat\ell_t+\frac{\eta^2}{8}.

Sum over all rounds. The logarithms telescope, leaving

ln⁡WT+1W1≤−ηL^T+η2T8. \ln\frac{W_{T+1}}{W_1} \le-\eta\widehat L_T+\frac{\eta^2T}{8}.

Combine the two bounds

Both inequalities describe the same quantity. Consequently,

−ηmin⁡iLi,T−ln⁡N≤−ηL^T+η2T8. -\eta\min_i L_{i,T}-\ln N \le-\eta\widehat L_T+\frac{\eta^2T}{8}.

Move the cumulative losses to one side and divide by the positive number η\eta:

L^T−min⁡iLi,T≤ln⁡Nη+ηT8. \widehat L_T-\min_i L_{i,T} \le\frac{\ln N}{\eta}+\frac{\eta T}{8}.

This proves the theorem. No probabilistic assumption about the loss sequence was used. In particular, the algebra still applies when an opponent chooses each loss vector after observing the current probability vector. That observation is what we will use in the minimax proof.

Reading the simulation

Open the expert-advice simulation and begin in randomized/Hedge mode. Step through one round at a time before running the whole sequence.

  • Weights: the bars show the normalized weights used for the next round. A mistake reduces an expert’s unnormalized weight; normalization changes everyone’s probability.
  • Learner loss: Hedge mode displays mixture loss, equal to conditional expected mistakes under the stated prediction protocol. No random expert is sampled.
  • Best expert: the comparison curve is the smallest cumulative expert loss at each prefix. Each comparison uses one fixed expert for that entire prefix.
  • Regret: subtract the best-expert total from the learner total. Regret may decrease between rounds. In this simulation’s Hedge mode, equal initial weights and a fixed learning rate keep cumulative mixture regret nonnegative. In deterministic Weighted Majority mode, regret can be negative.

The steady-signal preset shows weight concentrating on a successful rule. The regime-switch preset shows a limitation of the comparator: competing with the best fixed expert is weaker than competing with whichever expert is best at each moment. Compare learning rates on the same preset to see how aggressively past mistakes affect the next distribution.

The simulation is a finite example, while the theorem applies to every valid loss sequence. Its rounded horizon-tuned rate may differ slightly from the exact formula. Changing the setup resets the experiment so a single run uses one fixed rate.

What we will use next

The proof gives a general guarantee for combining finitely many options under bounded losses. In Part 2, those options become the rows of a game matrix. The opponent selects a column, and its entries become the experts’ losses. Average regret approaching zero will force minimax equality.
